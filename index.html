DeepScope — Telescope Control & Integration

Learning from real engineering experience

Meeting with Eng. Mario Magdy, who worked on DeepScope’s control system.

We already have the main parts of our autonomous telescope: mechanical structure, motors, drivers, sensors and an embedded controller. But having all the components does not mean having a working system. The real challenge starts when everything is connected.

I want to understand the practical problems of DeepScope’s development — motor control, positioning accuracy, calibration, feedback and integration — and avoid repeating problems that are not obvious in the first design.

01. Understanding the Mechanical System

Mechanical performance sets the ceiling for control accuracy. A perfect controller cannot fix a loose gear or a wobbling mount.

Torque must beat friction plus the load from any unbalanced weight. If the center of gravity sits away from the elevation axis, the motor fights gravity at every angle, so a counterweight is cheaper than a bigger motor. Gear ratio multiplies torque and resolution, but adds backlash: lost motion when direction reverses. Add structural vibration and resonance (stepper motors often shake badly at certain speeds) and mount stability decides everything.

  • How did you estimate the required motor torque?
  • Did the real load differ from your calculations?
  • Did backlash affect pointing accuracy?
  • Did you see vibration or resonance?
  • How did you balance the tube on the mount?
  • Which mechanical issue hurt the control system most?

02. From Target Angles to Motor Movement

The mount needs an Azimuth and Elevation angle. The controller turns each angle into motor steps:

steps = angle × (motor steps/rev × microstepping × gear ratio) ÷ 360°

Speed is the step rate. Acceleration and deceleration ramps stop the motor from stalling or shaking the mount. Open-loop assumes every step happened; closed-loop checks.

Assumed values (not DeepScope’s real ones). Edit them:

  • How did you convert Az/El into steps?
  • Open-loop, or encoder feedback?
  • Overshoot? How did you ramp speed?
  • Axes independent or synchronized?

03. Understanding the Control Loop

Open-loop:

Target→Controller→Motor→Telescope moves

Closed-loop:

Target→Controller→Motor→Actual position↩ feedback

The controller computes error = target − actual (from an encoder, say) and adjusts motor speed. A PID controller mixes present error (P), accumulated error (I) and error trend (D). Tune badly and you get overshoot, oscillation, or a very slow settling time. The goal is stability.

  • Did you implement PID?
  • Controlled variable: position or velocity?
  • What was the feedback source?
  • How did you tune it? Any oscillations?
  • Was PID actually necessary?
Important: PID is not automatically required just because a system contains motors. First, understand what variable needs to be controlled and what feedback is available.

04. Knowing Where the Telescope Is

Steppers have no memory of position. Before autonomous pointing, the system needs a reference: homing drives each axis into a limit switch (the mechanical zero), then calibration offsets correct the rest. Good homing is repeatable. Soft limits (in software) stop moves early; hard limits (switches) cut the motor as the last defense. After a power loss mid-move, the position is unknown, so re-homing is needed.

  • How did you set the initial reference?
  • Limit switches or encoders? Was homing repeatable?
  • How did you avoid exceeding mechanical limits?
  • What happens if power is lost during movement?
  • How did you calibrate Az and El?

05. From the Sky to the Telescope

Catalogs give RA and Dec, which are fixed on the sky. The mount needs Azimuth and Altitude, which depend on the observer’s latitude, longitude and UTC time. Because the Earth rotates, Alt/Az changes constantly, so an Alt-Az mount must keep moving both axes to track.

RA / Dec→Astronomy calc→Alt / Az→Motor angles→Motor commands

Trouble spots: wrong clock or GPS fix, frame mismatch, axis offsets, tracking rate, targets near the zenith (Az swings very fast), and limits near the horizon.

  • How did you convert RA/Dec to Alt/Az? Which libraries?
  • How did you account for Earth’s rotation?
  • Any transformation problems?
  • How accurate was the first pointing, and what limited it?

06. Using the Camera to Improve Pointing

The camera can close the loop: capture → detect object → compare with image center → compute error → move motors → capture again to verify. Pixel offset becomes angle through the camera’s field of view (degrees per pixel). Latency matters: if you correct faster than images arrive, the mount oscillates. Use thresholds and a handler for lost targets.

Capture→Detect→Compare to center→Error→Correct→Verify
  • Camera for detection only, or pointing correction too?
  • Plate solving?
  • Pixels to angular correction?
  • Did latency affect motor control? How did you avoid oscillating?
  • What happens when the target is lost?

07. When Everything Starts Working Together

Parts that work alone behave differently together. Stay split between high-level calculation (Pi / mobile) and real-time motor control (ESP32).

A. Electrical

Power supply sizing, motor current spikes, voltage drops, noise, grounding, driver heat, ESP32 resets, compass/IMU interference.

  • Did current spikes affect other electronics?
  • How did you isolate motor power from sensitive electronics?
  • Did noise hit the compass or IMU? Driver current limits?

B. Embedded & communication

Who does what, protocol choice, Wi-Fi latency, lost commands, task timing, error handling.

  • How did you split work between MCU and main computer?
  • A command arrives while motors are moving: what then?
  • Communication drops? Sensor reads vs motor timing?

C. Full system

Mobile app↓Raspberry Pi↓ESP32↓Motor drivers↓Stepper motors
Sensors: IMU, GPS, compass, switches

↑ feedback to the ESP32 ↑

The ESP32 stays responsible for step timing, so a slow image or Wi-Fi hiccup can’t make a motor stutter.

08. Lessons Learned from DeepScope

I’d like to hear how it really went. What was the hardest integration problem? Which part worked alone but failed in the full system? What design decision had to change? What took longest to debug? What would you change if you started again, and what should we test before assembling everything?

The most valuable engineering knowledge often comes from problems that were never mentioned in the original design.

09. What Should We Build and Test First?

Test every subsystem alone before integrating.

  1. Test motors and drivers independently
  2. Verify homing and limit switches
  3. Validate Azimuth and Elevation movement
  4. Test positioning accuracy
  5. Integrate astronomical calculations
  6. Implement basic tracking
  7. Integrate camera-based corrections
  8. Test complete autonomous operation

  • What should our first successful prototype demonstrate?
